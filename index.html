Weekly Readings

  • The story is a frame-tale; a spy thriller inside historical fiction.
  • The novel and the labyrinth are revealed to be one and the same construct, just taking different forms.
  • Unlike a typical narrative where a character picks one option and moves forward, Ts'ui Pên's novel makes every imaginable choice simultaneously.
  • This makes the book nearly impossible to follow linearly, much like house of leaves, it's "broken" by design.
  • Turning the pages lets you make individual choices without losing access to any other choice; past, present, or future.
  • This connects to git branching: every commit is a fork, every branch a parallel path that doesn't erase the others.
  • Net artists use intentional malfunction, misdirection, and interface breakdown to disrupt the user's sense of control and mastery.
  • This creates critical distance from technology; making the invisible infrastructure visible.
  • White warns the strategy risks becoming an empty style once absorbed into the art establishment.
  • Jodi: pioneers who deliberately broke HTML to make the web's hidden code visible.
  • Peter Luining mashed up computer games and abstract painting to disorient viewers and deny clear interaction.
  • Michaël Samyn used painterly visuals and intentionally counter-intuitive interfaces to critique computer conventions.
  • Broken or unexpected interfaces aren't bugs to fix; they're the work itself.
  • The 404 as aesthetic object rather than error: failure states can be intentional, even desirable outcomes.
  • LeWitt's wall drawing instructions read almost exactly like pseudocode, breaking a visual problem into simple, ordered steps before any actual implementation.
  • If three people apply the same structure and get wildly different results, what is the "work" exactly: the instruction, the output, or the process?
  • That the mouse interaction added to Wall Drawing #106 with "one line of code" producing a moiré pattern felt like a genuinely radical idea hiding inside a trivial change.
  • Separating concept from application gives creative freedom.
  • You can develop an idea fully in language before worrying about syntax.
  • Could you write instructions vague enough that two classmates working with the same brief get genuinely different, equally valid results?
  • The comparison of programming languages to art materials reframes the way I think about them. Going from strict and rigid to experimental and only limited by what you can think to do with them.
  • If natural language descriptions are more open and expressive than code, does that mean our programs are always a reduction of an idea and never its full expression?

  • The mystic programmer idea makes me wonder what role accidents and bugs play as creative tools rather than strictly problems to fix.
